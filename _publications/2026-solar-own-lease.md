title: "Willingness to pay for solar adoption: Own vs Lease"
authors:
  - "Sachin B. Badole"
  - "[Martin D. Heintzelman](https://www.udel.edu/academics/colleges/canr/departments/applied-economics-and-statistics/faculty-staff/martin-heintzelman/){:target=\"_blank\"}"
type: "working paper"
order: 2
abstract: >
  The availability of alternative financing arrangements has fundamentally reshaped the residential rooftop solar market in the United States (U.S.). Third-party ownership (TPO), including 25-year term leasing contracts and power purchase agreements (PPAs), allows households to adopt a solar system with little or no upfront investment. This has been promoted as a mechanism to expand access, particularly among low-income households. However, empirical evidence on households’ preferences and willingness to pay (WTP) for solar ownership versus leasing remains limited. This study provides new evidence on household preferences and WTP for solar adoption by explicitly comparing ownership and leasing contracts within a stated-preference framework. We designed a Best-to-Worst Discrete Choice Experiment (BWDCE) that presents different solar options (e.g., solar ownership versus lease, ownership versus ownership, and lease versus lease), in which respondents evaluate pairs of solar options and rank them Best-to-Worst. We vary key system attributes: down payment, monthly payment, total system cost, expected electricity bill savings, battery storage, variability in electricity generation, and reduction in carbon footprint. In addition, we collect detailed information on respondents’ attitudes towards solar systems, sociodemographic characteristics, financial conditions, and electricity bills. We implement a nationwide online survey using Qualtrics and collect data from 900 U.S. homeowners who have not adopted rooftop solar. We use a mixed logit and rank ordered logit model to estimate households' preferences and WTP for solar systems, allowing for unobserved preference heterogeneity. The preliminary results suggest that homeowners’ first-best choice is solar ownership, whereas their second-best choice is leasing. On average, they are willing to pay a total cost of around $18,700 to own a solar system and $12,100 under a leasing agreement (with incentives). This is closely aligned with the average cost of a 7-10 kW solar system (with incentives) in most U.S. states. The respondents' adoption decisions are primarily driven by financial and system attributes: they prefer monthly payments over down payments, place substantial value on expected electricity bill savings, strongly prefer systems with battery storage, and are less likely to choose systems with high variability in electricity generation or higher system costs. The choice to purchase a system is strongly associated with being male, younger, educated, the presence of children in the household, low- to medium-income status, and a liberal political leaning. Household financial conditions (current savings or investments), power outages, and geographic location also significantly influence decisions to adopt a solar system. Lastly, households with higher electricity bills are more likely to own solar, and WTP is higher compared to households with low electricity bills. This study contributes to the literature on residential solar adoption by providing a comprehensive, contract-specific WTP estimate that distinguishes between ownership and leasing decisions. The finding provides valuable guidelines for policymakers, solar developers, and other stakeholders to design targeted incentives, improve solar financing programs, and offer affordable solar systems to prospective consumers.
